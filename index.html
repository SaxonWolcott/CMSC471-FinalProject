Steam, Over Time

Steam launched in 2003 primarily as a way for Valve to provide updates to their games. In the two decades since then, Steam has revolutionized PC gaming and become the most successful game distributor in the world. Let's take a look at how its library has evolved over time and what kind of environment developers are facing today.

Setting the scale

Steam doesn't publish actual owner counts, so this project leans on SteamSpy — a third-party service that estimates how many people own each game. The estimates come in coarse ranges, so every game lands in one of five owner-count buckets, shown below. The rest of the page uses these colors and tier names everywhere.

Each circle is twice the radius (four times the area) of the previous tier — a compressed-but-readable proxy. The real ratios are even larger: a typical Phenomenon-tier game reaches roughly six thousand times the audience of a Drowned-tier one.

1. The Flood

In 2004, six games launched on Steam. In 2025, more than twenty-one thousand did. The bars below stack every year's releases by how big an audience each game found.

Both the proportion and the raw number of Drowned games rose dramatically in the years following Steam Greenlight in 2012, and kept climbing after Steam Direct replaced it in 2017. The trend has two likely drivers: better tools have made it possible for less-experienced developers to ship complete games, and Valve's progressively looser submission policies have let almost all of those games onto the store.

Owner counts are SteamSpy algorithmic estimates, not real sales figures, and come bucketed in coarse ranges. The two rightmost years (2024 and 2025) are still accumulating owners — older games had over a decade to find an audience, while a 2025 release has had less than one.

2. The Indie Wave

In 2008, about one in six Steam releases were classified as Indie. By 2018, more than three in four. Other genres tell their own stories underneath — Action quietly shrank, Casual and Adventure swelled. Click any genre on the right edge of the chart to highlight its trajectory.

The meaning of the label "indie" has shifted dramatically since it took hold. Today it stretches from Undertale — made almost entirely by one person — to Clair Obscur: Expedition 33, made by a 30-person studio that won Game of the Year. The same accessibility that lets anyone start making a game also means the catalog is more saturated than ever: a small indie project that someone spent a year of their life on is likely to drown among thousands of peers without a bit of luck.

These are Steam's publisher-set genres — distinct from the community-applied tags the project uses as filters in Section 4. Steam games typically carry multiple genres at once: a single title might list Action, Indie, and Adventure simultaneously. Each line shows the share of releases that included a given genre, not an exclusive split. Lines can cross and don't sum to 100%.

3. Will anyone see your game?

The first two scenes painted the catalog's growth in volume and composition. But for a developer releasing a game, only half of that picture matters — the other half is whether anyone ever sees it. The line below tracks the share of each year's releases that eventually accumulated 50 Steam reviews: a low bar, but a fair proxy for whether the discoverability machinery surfaced the title at all. Toggle to Ratings to see how the games that did find an audience are scored.

In 2014, about 9 in 10 Steam releases eventually crossed 50 reviews. By 2024, only about 1 in 5. Both Greenlight (2012) and especially Steam Direct (2017) preceded sharp drops in this share — but the cause isn't curation, it's volume. A 2024 release competed for attention against ten times more titles than a 2014 release did, and the audience didn't grow nearly as fast. That collapse is the baseline a new developer should plan around: ratings, when a game gets them, are still healthy — but most games don't get there.

"Reaching 50 reviews" is a discoverability proxy — below that bar, review ratios are noisy enough that quality signals aren't reliable. 2025 is excluded from the Discoverability view because games released that year haven't had time to accumulate reviews; the 2023 and 2024 figures will still edge upward as more time passes.
